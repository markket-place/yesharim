# Returning to the Path: Why We Need Ancient Wisdom

There is a particular kind of wisdom that does not necessarily tell us anything we have never heard before. Instead, it reminds us of what we already know, but so easily forget

In _Mesilat Yesharim_ (_The Path of the Just_), Rabbi Moshe Chaim Luzzatto, known as the Ramchal, begins by explaining that the foundations of human responsibility and spiritual growth are not obscure or inaccessible. Many of these principles are familiar to us. We know that gratitude is good, that kindness matters, that we should treat others with dignity, and that our actions have consequences. As Rabbi Ovadia of Bartenura's commentary on the Mishnah and other traditional teachers remind us in different ways, wisdom often begins with things that are already before our eyes

**Yet knowing something and living by it are two different things**

The generations before us often lived within communities, traditions, and daily practices that reinforced these values. Gratitude, generosity, and responsibility were not merely ideas to consider when convenient; they were habits cultivated through family, community, study, and ritual. We should not romanticize the past, as though previous generations were free from their own struggles and shortcomings. But there is something worth recovering in the recognition that a good life requires more than good intentions. It requires practice

Ramchal understood that even familiar truths can fade from our attention. When we are busy, distracted, anxious, or preoccupied with our immediate needs, we can forget to appreciate what we have. We can overlook opportunities to help someone else. We can allow resentment, gossip, and negativity to occupy the space where generosity and joy might otherwise grow. This is why learning, repetition, and deliberate self-examination are so important. We return to wisdom not simply to acquire information, but to shape the people we become

## Three Encounters with the Same Book

I have studied _Mesilat Yesharim_ at three different stages of my life

The first was when I was young, in Colombia. At that age, I approached the book primarily as a student, learning principles and trying to understand what they meant. My world was smaller, and many of the questions that would later become central to my life had not yet arrived

The second time was during my years in yeshiva in Jerusalem. There, I was surrounded by people who shared a commitment to learning, prayer, and personal growth. Discipline was not something I had to construct entirely on my own. The rhythm of the day, the expectations of the community, and the encouragement of friends helped reinforce the values we were studying. We reminded one another, sometimes without even realizing it, of the kind of people we wanted to become

The third encounter came after a long pause, as I found myself rediscovering the book in Los Angeles

Every time, the questions felt different

As we have more autonomy, we have to make decisions every day about work, money, food, relationships, where to live, how to spend time, and what kind of future to build. These decisions may seem ordinary, but together they shape our character and our lives. And without the same structures that once surrounded us, it becomes much easier to drift away from the values we sincerely believe in

When we are kids, our environment often helps us maintain our discipline. As adults, we must become more intentional about building that environment ourselves. We must choose what we pay attention to, whom we spend time with, which habits we cultivate, and what we allow to occupy our minds. Freedom brings possibility, but it also brings responsibility. The more choices we have, the more important it becomes to pause and ask whether those choices reflect the people we hope to be

Returning to _Mesilat Yesharim_ at this stage of life has reminded me that the work of becoming a better person is never finished. A teaching that once seemed obvious can reveal a new depth when we encounter it under different circumstances. The words may be the same, but the person reading them has changed

## A Practice of Remembering

This blog is not an attempt to present new wisdom or to suggest that ancient teachers left something essential undiscovered. Quite the opposite. It is an invitation to return to teachings that have sustained generations of people, and to consider how we might put them into practice in the circumstances of our own lives

We will explore gratitude, kindness, humility, discipline, responsibility, and _tikkun olam_—the work of repairing and improving our world. We will approach these ideas with respect for their Jewish roots and with an understanding that their ethical significance can speak to people across cultures, backgrounds, and stages of life

You do not need to have all the answers to begin. You do not need to be perfect, particularly religious, or especially learned. You need only a willingness to pay attention, to examine your actions honestly, and discipline develops naturally

Perhaps the simplest reason to return to these teachings is that they help us notice the beauty already present in our lives. Gratitude allows us to appreciate what we might otherwise take for granted. Kindness helps us recognize the needs of others. Discipline gives our values a place in our daily routines. Together, these practices can contribute to a life with greater happiness, well-being, and tranquility—not because they remove every difficulty, but because they change how we meet the world

There is also a quiet abundance that emerges when we begin looking for opportunities to help others. A person who is attentive to someone else's needs has less room to dwell on gossip, resentment, and needless negativity. A person who practices generosity discovers that wealth is not measured only in possessions or money, but also in the richness of one's thoughts, the care expressed through one's words, and the goodness embodied in one's actions

**Read, Reflect, Practice, Forget, and Return**
