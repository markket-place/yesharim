_Reflections on Parashat Chayei Sarah_

There are days when we wish we could simply fly away

Away from the arguments, the responsibilities, the inconveniences, and the endless little things that seem to demand our attention. Sometimes even ordinary interactions become exhausting. Someone says something unpleasant. Someone misunderstands us. Someone takes advantage of our generosity. Someone cuts in line

And suddenly, something that should have occupied thirty seconds occupies our thoughts for the rest of the afternoon

David HaMelech gives voice to this longing:

> מִי־יִתֶּן־לִּי אֵבֶר כַּיּוֹנָה אָעוּפָה וְאֶשְׁכֹּנָה
>
> _Who will give me wings like a dove? I would fly away and find rest._
>
> — Tehillim 55:7

Perhaps we know exactly what David means. We want a little peace. We want to stop carrying every frustration. We want to get away from the noise; but what if the wings we are asking for are not necessarily wings to escape our lives? **What if they are the ability to live those lives differently?**

To remain patient without becoming passive. To stand up for what is right without becoming cruel. To recognize our responsibilities toward others without being ruled by our own irritation

This week's parashah, _Chayei Sarah_, offers a mystical hint

## Avraham: Strength in generosity

The parashah opens with the death of Sarah. Avraham mourns his wife and then begins the difficult task of securing a burial place for her

He approaches the people of the land and asks to purchase the Cave of Machpelah; te negotiation with Ephron is interesting. Ephron initially offers the land as a gift, but Avraham insists on paying its full value. Eventually, he weighs out four hundred shekels of silver

There is grief, a practical problem, a negotiation, and a significant financial transaction—all happening at an emotionally difficult moment

Yet Avraham remains deliberate. He knows what he needs. He communicates clearly. He does not abandon his objective, and he remains generous

The Ramban, commenting on Genesis 23:4, explores Avraham's description of himself as both a stranger and a resident among the people of the land. Avraham recognizes his position in their society while making a serious request for a permanent burial place

Humility does not mean pretending we have no needs. Respect does not mean giving up what matters. And patience does not mean allowing every situation to be decided by someone else's wishes

**Avraham is determined to be kind**

## Rivkah: Kindness that goes beyond

Later, Avraham's servant arrives at a well in search of a wife for Yitzchak; he asks Hashem for a sign: the young woman who offers him water and also volunteers to water his camels will demonstrate the exceptional character he is looking for

Rivkah arrives; she gives him water, and then she does something more. She continues drawing water for the camels until they have finished drinking

> וַתְּמַהֵר וַתְּעַר כַּדָּהּ
>
> _She hurried and emptied her jug._
>
> — Genesis 24:20

Rivkah doesn't merely perform the minimum requested of her. She notices what else is needed and takes responsibility

Rivkah's generosity is not portrayed as resentment or obligation. She acts with initiative. She sees an opportunity to help and responds

## The danger of anger: When the ego becomes the center

The Rambam writes in _Hilchot De'ot_ 2:3 that anger is an exceptionally destructive character trait. He instructs us to distance ourselves from it, even to the opposite extreme

He cites the teaching of the Sages:

> כָּל הַכּוֹעֵס כְּאִלּוּ עוֹבֵד עֲבוֹדָה זָרָה
>
> _Whoever becomes angry is as though he worships idols._

Things must happen according to our expectations. People must behave as we believe they should. When they don't, we feel that something fundamental has been violated

But Hashem is the only power, not us

The _Iggeret HaRamban_ similarly warns against anger and encourages humility. It quotes the Talmud:

> כָּל הַכּוֹעֵס כָּל מִינֵי גֵיהִנָּם שׁוֹלְטִין בּוֹ
>
> _Whoever becomes angry, all kinds of Gehinnom hold sway over him._
>
> — Nedarim 22a

These teachings are not asking us to become indifferent to injustice. They are asking us to examine the inner force behind our reactions. **We should not confuse the ability to feel an emotion with the obligation to obey it**

Hitbodedut helps develop the humility and awareness to understand our actions and what guides them

## Silence is strong

The Sages teach:

> כָּל הַמַּעֲבִיר עַל מִדּוֹתָיו מַעֲבִירִין לוֹ עַל כָּל פְּשָׁעָיו
>
> _Whoever overlooks offenses committed against him, his transgressions are overlooked._
>
> — Rosh Hashanah 17a

We can say, _Kaparah, Hashem._ Let this be an atonement. Let me use this moment to strengthen my patience

And when speaking up is important; we can stablish boundaris without being unecesarily insulting or dehumanizing

## Yitzchak: The importance of finding a quiet place

Toward the end of the parashah, we encounter Yitzchak in the field:

> וַיֵּצֵא יִצְחָק לָשׂוּחַ בַּשָּׂדֶה
>
> _Yitzchak went out to meditate in the field._
>
> — Genesis 24:63

We can tell Hashem what is actually happening inside us; we can say that we are frustrated. That we are tired. That we are struggling with someone. That we regret something we said. That we don't know how to handle a situation

Rebbe Nachman describes this personal conversation as an essential part of one's relationship with Hashem in _Likutei Moharan_ II:25

In _Mesilat Yesharim_, chapter 3, the Ramchal teaches the importance of _zehirut_: watchfulness, careful attention to our actions, and the willingness to examine where our choices are leading us

**We look honestly at our behavior, and then we speak to Hashem about it**
